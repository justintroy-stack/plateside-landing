How it works

Five steps, and the last one repeats.

  1. Tell it your kitchen

    How you eat, what you cook on, who you're feeding. Carnivore, vegan, whatever's real for you — any regimen, any appliance.

  2. Builds a rotation

    Real dinners you'd actually cook, sized to your goal.

  3. Tracks your stock

    As you log meals, and tells you what to buy, at which store, and when.

  4. Add a lab report, if you have one

    Skip it and nothing breaks — the rotation already works from your kitchen and your goal. Add a report and food targets sharpen: researched guideline targets, not a guess, each one with a source.

  5. Test again

    Dinner re-shapes itself, and a change waits for the food you already bought to run out first, so nothing is stranded.

Why this exists

"Eat better" isn't an instruction. It's a homework assignment with no due date.

The gap

Dinner needs deciding three hundred times a year. Nothing tells you what's actually running low, what to buy for it, or which store to buy it at — you're rebuilding that from memory every single week. "Eat healthier" doesn't answer any of it, even when it's the right advice.

The cost

If you've had blood work done, you already paid for it — a panel, a copay, sometimes hundreds out of pocket. What it should change for dinner has been free advice ever since, which is why it's usually vague.

Your data

Your results stay yours.

Nothing you type or upload ever leaves your phone — including a lab report. No account. No server. Whoever builds this can't see your results, because there's no place to see them from.
